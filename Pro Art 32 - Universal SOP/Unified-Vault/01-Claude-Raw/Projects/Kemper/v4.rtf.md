This position is legally barred by a doctrine called "equitable estoppel." Explained in plain language, with no assumed legal knowledge:WHAT ESTOPPEL MEANSEstoppel is the legal principle that prevents a party from taking a new position that contradicts an earlier position, when the other party has reasonably relied on the earlier position to their detriment. The simplest way to understand it: once you have committed to a position in writing and the other person has acted on it, you cannot reverse course to harm them.A real-world example: Imagine you ask a contractor for a quote on roof repair. They say $5,000. You hire them based on that quote, fire higher-priced bidders, and they begin work. Halfway through the job, they say, "Actually, we never agreed to $5,000. The price is now $15,000." The law will not let them collect the higher price. They committed to $5,000 in writing, you relied on it, and you would be harmed if they reversed the position. That is estoppel.HOW ESTOPPEL APPLIES TO KEMPER'S POSITIONThe four elements of equitable estoppel under California law are:   (1) The party to be estopped (Kemper) must know the facts;   (2) The party to be estopped must intend its conduct to be relied upon (or act in a way the other party reasonably relied on);   (3) The other party (me) must not know the true facts (or must be entitled to rely on the conduct);   (4) The other party must rely on the conduct to their injury.Source authority for these elements: City of Long Beach v.
