**Plain Statement of the Issue** 

This matter is straightforward.  
I paid a merchant for a Done-For-You (DFY) Airbnb acquisition and deployment service that was clearly promised, clearly defined, and never delivered.

The merchant accepted payment, failed to deliver the promised services, missed all agreed timelines, and later attempted to justify non-delivery by asserting “terms” and billing concepts that were **never presented, never agreed to, and never signed** by me.

Those post-payment terms are **unenforceable as a matter of law** and cannot be used to defeat a services-not-rendered claim.

Despite this, First Interstate Bank has treated the merchant’s unilateral, unsigned assertions as if they were governing contractual terms \- even though no valid agreement exists reflecting those provisions.

I have attached extensive, undeniable documentation originating from the merchant itself \- including advertising, invoices, sales representations, written communications, and recorded statements \- all of which establish:

* **what was promised**

* **that payment was made**

* **that delivery never occurred**

This is not a quality dispute or a misunderstanding.

This is a documented services-not-rendered dispute involving misrepresentation and fraudulent inducement.

Under consumer protection law and Mastercard dispute standards, a merchant cannot cure non-delivery by inventing or retroactively enforcing terms that were never agreed to, and a bank cannot rely on such terms to deny a valid dispute.

I am requesting that First Interstate Bank correct this matter immediately by refunding the remaining $10,500, plus all associated interest and fees, consistent with federal consumer credit law and network dispute rules.

## **1.4.A) Preparatory Activity Cannot Satisfy the Essential Purpose of the Contract**

Even assuming analytics research is a preparatory component of the workflow, preparatory steps do not equal delivery of the contracted service  
.  
The transaction was not for consulting, research assistance, or software access.  
The transaction was for a Done-For-You Airbnb acquisition and deployment resulting in an operational, furnished, revenue-generating property.

Under contract law, performance is measured by fulfillment of the **essential purpose** of the agreement \- not by isolated preparatory activity.

See:  
 	 • Restatement (Second) of Contracts §235  
	 • UCC failure of consideration principles  
	 • Texas contract law on essential purpose and material breach

The essential purpose required:

 • acquisition of a landlord-approved property  
	 • furnishing and interior deployment  
	 • listing activation  
	 • operational launch  
	 • revenue generation capability

None of these occurred.

Where the essential purpose of a contract fails, preparatory work cannot convert non-performance into performance.

Courts consistently reject the argument that incomplete preparation satisfies a contract whose central objective was never achieved.  
At most, analytics access represents abandoned preliminary activity that never matured into delivery.

This is legally classified as:

 • failure of consideration  
	 • material breach  
	 • services not rendered  
	 • unjust enrichment if payment is retained

A party cannot retain full payment by pointing to incomplete preparation when the contracted result was never delivered.

That principle applies equally in contract law and card network dispute frameworks.

## **1.6.A) KEY LEGAL POINT: Unenforceable Post-Payment Terms**

A contract requires mutual assent.

The merchant has produced no signed agreement, no acceptance record, and no contemporaneous disclosure showing that the consumer agreed to the “terms” now being asserted.

Terms introduced after payment \- or never disclosed at all \- are legally unenforceable and cannot be used to:

* justify non-delivery  
* convert non-performance into performance  
* defeat consumer protection rights

Treating unilateral, unsigned merchant assertions as binding terms contradicts basic contract law principles and cannot support a valid dispute denial.

Additionally, even if a merchant claims “terms exist,” the operative question is whether there is **evidence of mutual assent at or before payment** (signature, clickwrap acceptance record, or contemporaneous disclosure tied to the transaction). 

The merchant has provided no enforceable acceptance record for the post-payment terms now being asserted. Therefore, those terms cannot defeat a services-not-rendered claim or convert non-performance into performance.

* **Statutory damages** (subject to statutory limits in 15 U.S.C. §1640)  
* **Court costs**  
* **Reasonable attorney’s fees** (fee shifting upon prevailing)

* **actual damages exposure**,
