# Complaint Narrative

This complaint concerns First Interstate Bank’s reliance on merchant-post-submitted terms that merchant admits were **initially omitted,** that may not constitute **a legally enforceable agreement** when **denying** a billing-error dispute. The issue presented is whether the issuer relied on **unsigned** and **previously undisclosed merchant terms** \- produced **after** the transaction; without **verifying** whether those terms were ever clearly presented to or **unambiguously** **affirmatively agreed to by the consumer**.

A **creditor’s obligation under federal law** is not merely to collect a merchant response, but to conduct an independent and **meaningful investigation** of the billing-error claim before determining whether the charge is valid.

This complaint concerns whether First Interstate Bank conducted the **meaningful** **and** **reasonable investigation** **required** under the **Fair Credit Billing Act** and **Regulation Z** before denying a billing-error dispute. The bank’s determination appears to rely on merchant-submitted **one-sided** terms that were **initially omitted**, **currently unsigned**, and produced **months** after the **initial transaction and payment**; which does not constitute a **legally enforceable agreement**.

Under the **Fair Credit Billing Act (15 U.S.C. §1666)**, when a creditor receives written notice of a billing error, the creditor must investigate the matter and correct the error if one exists. This statute governs credit-card billing disputes and **establishes the issuer’s duty to conduct a meaningful investigation** rather than **relying** **solely** on merchant assertions.

**Regulation Z**, which implements the **Fair Credit Billing Act**, **requires** that a **creditor conduct a reasonable investigation of the consumer’s claim before denying** a billing error.

During the investigation of the disputed charge, the bank relied on merchant-submitted “terms and agreement” documents as a primary basis for denying the billing error. However, there is **no evidence** that those alleged terms were ever clearly presented to or affirmatively accepted by the consumer at the time of the transaction.

There is also **documented evidence** **provided** by **BOTH** the merchant and the cardholder, used and possessed by First Interstate Bank (FIB); showing that the merchant writes in text roughly 1 month after the transaction, that the **merchant omitted the terms and agreement** **at time of purchase**.

- This has been **documented** and **verified** through many channels, such as certified mail, phone, fax, prior dated threads in writing, etc.  
- This **admission is critical** because it **confirms** that the **merchant’s alleged terms and agreement were not presented to the consumer at the time of purchase**. **Without clear notice of the terms** and **an unambiguous manifestation of assent**, contract law generally recognizes that **no binding agreement to those terms exists**.

Courts consistently hold that online contractual terms are enforceable only where consumers receive reasonably conspicuous notice of the terms and **clearly manifest assent to those terms**.

If either element is missing \- **clear notice** or **unambiguous agreement** \- courts find that **no binding contract exists**.

This principle has been repeatedly applied by federal courts evaluating website terms and conditions, including, but not limited to ***Specht v. Netscape Communications Corp.***, ***Nguyen v. Barnes** & **Noble Inc.***, and ***Berman v. Freedom Financial Network, LLC**., etc.*

In this matter, the merchant’s rebuttal relied on omitted, alleged terms created by the merchant **after** **payment** to justify **non-delivery** of the **original promised services** that are uniform across ads, marketing material, sales call transcript, website, merchant invoice, merchant writing and documentation, etc. 

However, there is **no evidence** that these **omitted post-payment** terms were clearly presented to or **unambiguously accepted & agreed to by the consumer** during the purchase process. (**lack of mutual assent**)

Screenshots from the merchant’s website also show that links to the merchant’s “Terms” and “Privacy Policy” pages return 404 “Page Not Found” errors, **further solidifying** that **terms were inaccessible to consumers at the time of purchase**.

Despite these issues, First Interstate Bank appears to have accepted the merchant’s non-contractual defenses without independently evaluating whether a **valid** agreement containing those terms was ever formed; while simultaneously **ignoring** **cardholder’s** **evidence** and **statements** **proving** **no mutually assenting, and/or signed agreement exists**.

Merchant has deceptively created unsigned terms that are significantly and materially different than the documented ads, sales, website, invoice, and material in writing uniformly affirm. This bait and switch tactic is considered deception defined by the **FTC**, as well as being clearly defined as **deceptive acts and practices** \- that **FIB** is **supporting** and **enforcing**.

Where a merchant’s explanation relies on materially different terms **initially omitted**, and introduced **after** the transaction; a reasonable investigation should evaluate whether those terms were ever presented to or **unambiguously agreed to** by the consumer before relying on them as the basis for denying a billing-error claim.

Where a creditor relies on merchant assertions without verifying whether those assertions are legally valid or supported by the **evidence** **submitted** **by the consumer**, the investigation may **fail to satisfy** the **reasonable-investigation** **requirement** imposed by the **Fair Credit Billing Act** and **Regulation Z.**

# Federal Compliance Framework

Federal law establishes clear obligations for credit-card issuers when a consumer disputes a charge.

Under the **Fair Credit Billing Act (15 U.S.C. §1666)**, when a creditor receives written notice of a billing error, the creditor must investigate the matter and correct the error if one exists.

**Regulation Z**, which implements the **Fair Credit Billing Act**, further provides:

“The creditor must conduct a reasonable investigation of the consumer’s claim.”  
**12 CFR §1026.13(f)**

**Regulation Z** also defines a billing error to include:

“A reflection on a statement of goods or services not accepted by the consumer or not delivered as agreed.”  
**12 CFR §1026.13(a)(3)**

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau’s official interpretation clarifies that **a creditor may not deny a billing-error claim solely because a merchant asserts that the charge is valid**.

Accordingly, **a creditor must independently evaluate the evidence submitted by the consumer** **and** **determine whether the goods or services were actually delivered as agreed rather than simply accepting the merchant’s narrative**.

Where a creditor relies on merchant representations or contractual defenses without independently evaluating whether those defenses are **legally valid**, this raises concerns regarding **compliance** with the **investigation** **requirements** of the **Fair Credit Billing Act** and **Regulation Z**.

**Failure to conduct a meaningful investigation** may also raise concerns under the **Consumer Financial Protection Act (12 U.S.C. §5531)**, which **prohibits unfair practices in consumer financial services** when **consumers suffer substantial financial injury that they cannot reasonably avoid**.

Failure to comply with the investigation obligations imposed by the **Fair Credit Billing Act** may also create **liability** under the **Truth in Lending Act (15 U.S.C. §1640)**.

---

**Issuer Accountability**

Credit-card issuers are responsible for ensuring that billing-error determinations are based on a **reasonable investigation** of the facts. Where a bank relies on merchant representations that **contradict evidence** provided by the consumer, **fails to verify the legal validity** of merchant “contractual” defenses, or denies a claim without independently evaluating whether services were delivered **as agreed**, such conduct may violate the investigation obligations imposed by the **Fair Credit Billing Act** and **Regulation Z**.

**\*\***These **obligations** exist specifically to **prevent financial institutions from enforcing charges that arise from misrepresentation, deception, or non-delivery of promised goods or services.\*\***

# Investigation Concerns

In this dispute, the bank appears to have relied on merchant assertions regarding internal work activity and alleged **one-way**, **unsigned,** **post** **agreement** terms \- presented by the merchant months after the chargeback and initial transaction date; rather than evaluating whether the purchased service that the was advertised **at the time of purchase** was actually delivered as agreed. (mutual assent)

The merchant and the issuer bear the burden of producing substantial evidence to prove that the consumer **unambiguously and affirmatively** **manifested** **assent** to those terms, as the courts and law require.

The disputed charge relates to a fixed-fee service program promising the acquisition, design, furnishing, listing, and launch of an operational profiting Airbnb property. However, the promised service outcome was never delivered.

Internal work activity claimed by the merchant does not satisfy delivery of the purchased service where the transaction was marketed and sold as a fixed-fee program tied to a specific outcome. (abundance of documented proof of evidence provided, including issuer’s affirmed possession of this proof)

If the merchant’s omitted alleged post-transaction terms were never validly presented or accepted **unambiguously** during the purchase process, reliance on those terms to justify denial of the billing-error dispute are **not legally binding**.

Even if the merchant attempted to introduce new terms after the transaction, any modification of the agreement would require clear notice of the new terms and **unambiguous** **affirmative acceptance by the consumer**. The available record contains no evidence of such **unambiguous** acceptance.

**Partial Refund / “Downscale” Does Not Establish Contractual Agreement**

The issuer’s determination appears to rely on the merchant’s decision to issue a partial refund based on a unilateral “downscale” of the service from two properties to one property after missed timelines and non-delivery of the original service scope clearly communicated through documented text messages and emails to the team. (proof provided and attached)

However, the merchant’s unilateral decision to partially refund the transaction or reduce the scope of the service does not establish that the consumer unambiguously agreed to new contractual terms governing the transaction.

A merchant’s statement that “no further refunds will be provided” does not constitute evidence of mutual agreement. Contract law requires clear notice of the proposed terms and an **unambiguous manifestation of assent by the consumer** before those terms become binding.

A unilateral statement by the merchant regarding refund limitations or modified service scope cannot, by itself, establish that the consumer accepted those terms. Without **clear evidence of affirmative agreement**, such statements remain unilateral conditions rather than mutually agreed contractual terms.

A **reasonable** **investigation** under **Regulation Z** should therefore evaluate whether the **consumer ever clearly agreed** to the merchant’s post-transaction refund conditions before relying on those conditions as the basis for denying the billing-error dispute.

The merchant’s later decision to reduce the scope of the service after **documented communication** on **missed timelines** and **non-delivery of the original agreement;** does not change the fact that the service purchased and **advertised** **at the time of payment** was **never delivered as promised**.

Absent evidence that the consumer affirmatively accepted the modified terms, the merchant’s post-transaction conditions regarding refunds or scope reduction cannot reasonably be treated as a binding contractual modification.

A reasonable investigation would therefore include evaluating whether:

* those terms were reasonably **conspicuous** to the consumer, **AND**  
* the consumer **unambiguously and affirmatively** **manifested** **assent** to those terms

The available record raises concerns that the bank accepted the merchant’s contractual defenses without verifying whether those legal prerequisites for contract formation were satisfied.

*The bank’s determination appears to treat the consumer’s continued communication with the merchant during attempts to resolve the issue as acceptance of newly asserted contractual terms. However, contract law requires a **clear and unambiguous manifestation of assent** before contractual terms become binding. Continued communication or attempts to resolve a dispute **do not constitute affirmative agreement** to new unsigned terms presented by the merchant post-transaction.*

Continued engagement with the merchant during attempts to resolve delays, non-delivery, or missed timelines does not constitute authorization for billable hours or acceptance of a new hourly service arrangement. The transaction was advertised and sold as a fixed-fee service program promising the acquisition and launch of an operational Airbnb property. There is **no evidence** that the consumer agreed to convert the transaction into an hourly consulting engagement or authorized billable hours as a substitute for delivery of the promised service outcome.

Accordingly, a reasonable investigation should evaluate whether the consumer ever agreed to an hourly billing structure before relying on the merchant’s claimed internal work activity as justification for denying the billing-error dispute.

Accordingly, the investigation must identify what evidence demonstrates that the consumer was provided clear notice of the merchant’s omitted alleged terms and **unambiguously manifested assent to those terms** at the time of initial purchase/payment, before they were relied upon to deny the billing-error dispute.

**Questions First Interstate Bank continues to avoid, despite many documented explicit requests for information:**

The sequence of events raises three distinct issues that should be evaluated during a reasonable investigation:

1\. Whether the merchant’s **omitted** alleged contractual terms were **clearly** presented to the consumer at the time of purchase.

2\. Whether the consumer **clearly** and **affirmatively, unambiguously** **agreed** to those terms.

3\. Whether the promised service outcome was **actually** **delivered** **as advertised** and **agreed**.

The available record raises concerns regarding all three.
