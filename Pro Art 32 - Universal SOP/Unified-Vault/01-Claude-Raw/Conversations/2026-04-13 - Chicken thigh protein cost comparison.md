# Bone-in wins — but barely

| Metric | Bone-in, skin-on @ $6.99/lb | Boneless, skinless @ $9.99/lb |
|---|---|---|
| Raw weight | 454 g | 454 g |
| Edible meat yield | ~65% → ~295 g | ~100% → ~454 g |
| Protein (raw, ~20g/100g) | ~59 g | ~91 g |
| **Cost per gram protein** | **~$0.118** | **~$0.110** |
| Cost per 100g protein | ~$11.80 | ~$11.00 |

## Verdict
**Boneless is actually ~7% cheaper per gram of protein** once you back out bone and skin waste. Close call, but boneless edges it.

## Caveats
- Yield assumption (65% edible on bone-in) is the swing variable. If your thighs are meatier (70%+), bone-in ties or wins.
- Skin adds fat calories, not protein — it doesn't help the protein math.
- Bone-in has upside if you're using the bones for stock or the skin for crisping/fat rendering (functional value beyond protein).
- Boneless saves prep time — worth something if you're hitting 200–216g/day consistently.

**Bottom line for your macros:** at these exact prices, grab the boneless. If bone-in drops to ~$6.49 or lower, it flips.

# Chicken skin — honest breakdown

## What's actually in it
Per 1 oz (28g) of chicken skin, roughly:
- **Calories:** ~130
- **Fat:** ~12g (about 55% of that is monounsaturated — same family as olive oil)
- **Protein:** ~3g
- **Carbs:** 0g
- **Micronutrients:** trace — small amounts of selenium, B vitamins, collagen

## The real story
**Fat profile is better than its reputation.** Chicken skin fat is roughly 55% monounsaturated, 30% saturated, 15% polyunsaturated. The mono portion is the same fat type praised in olive oil and avocados.

**Collagen is minimal for joint support.** Skin has some, but if you're after collagen for recovery/joints, bone broth or a collagen peptide powder delivers dramatically more per serving.

**Not a protein play.** At ~3g protein per 130 calories, skin is a fat source, not a protein source. Don't count it toward your 200–216g goal.

## For your context specifically
Given your chronic inflammatory sensitivity and high-protein targets:

- **Pro:** Clean fat source, satiating, zero carbs, adds flavor without seed oils
- **Con:** Calorie-dense (~460 cal/100g) — easy to overshoot if you're tracking for "Goku shredded"
- **Watch:** How it's cooked matters more than the skin itself. Pan-rendered in its own fat = fine. Deep-fried in seed oils = inflammatory load you don't want.

## Verdict
**Eat it if you enjoy it, don't force it down for "nutrition."** It's a decent fat source but not a functional food. If you're lean-bulking or cutting, the calories may not be worth it versus leaner thigh meat + a dedicated fat source (olive oil, avocado) you control more precisely.

# Boiling bone-in, skin-on chicken thighs

## Quick method (for eating the meat)

1. **Place thighs in a pot**, single layer, cover with cold water by ~1 inch
2. **Add salt** — 1 tsp per quart of water (plus aromatics if you want: garlic, bay leaf, peppercorns, onion)
3. **Bring to a boil**, then immediately drop to a low simmer (barely bubbling)
4. **Simmer 25–30 minutes** until internal temp hits **165°F** at the thickest part near the bone
5. **Rest 5 minutes** before pulling/shredding

## Key rules

- **Start in cold water** — gives even cooking, prevents rubbery exterior
- **Never hard boil** — a rolling boil makes the meat tough and stringy. Low simmer only.
- **Skim the foam** off the top in the first 5 minutes for cleaner broth
- **Don't dump the liquid** — that's bone broth. Strain and save it.

## Bonus: you get two things at once

| Output | How to use |
|---|---|
| **Shredded chicken** | Meal prep, salads, tacos, high-protein bowls |
| **Chicken broth** | Sip, cook rice in it, soup base, free collagen + minerals |

## Protein optimization tip
For your 200–216g/day target: 4 bone-in thighs (~1.5 lbs raw) yields roughly **~60–70g protein** of cooked, shredded meat. Pair with the broth for satiety with minimal extra calories.

## If you want crispy skin instead
Boiling kills the skin texturally — it goes rubbery. If crispy skin matters to you, **don't boil** — roast at 425°F for 35–40 min or air-fry at 400°F for 22–25 min instead. Boiling is best when you're going to shred the meat and discard or render the skin separately.

What happens if you don’t skim the top

---

## 🤖 Claude

# Nothing dangerous — just cosmetic and flavor

## What that foam actually is
It's **denatured protein (mostly albumin)** pushed out of the meat as it heats, mixed with trace blood, fat, and impurities. Not harmful. Not toxic. Fully edible.

## What happens if you leave it

| Impact | Result |
|---|---|
| **Broth clarity** | Cloudy, grayish instead of golden and clear |
| **Flavor** | Slightly muddier, less clean-tasting — the foam can carry a faint "off" or metallic note |
| **Texture** | Tiny gritty specks may settle or coat the meat |
| **Nutrition** | Essentially unchanged — you're not losing protein that matters |
| **Safety** | Zero issue |

## Bottom line
If you're **eating the meat and tossing the liquid** → skip skimming, doesn't matter.

If you're **keeping the broth to drink or cook with** → skim it. Takes 30 seconds and the difference in taste and appearance is noticeable. Professional kitchens skim because clean broth = clean flavor base.

**Pro move:** Skim once at the 3–5 minute mark when foam peaks, then again briefly around 10 minutes. After that it stops forming.

# Taste impact — subtle but real

## What unskimmed broth tastes like
- **Muddier, heavier mouthfeel** — less clean on the palate
- **Faint metallic or "livery" note** from the blood proteins in the foam
- **Slightly bitter edge** in the background, especially as it reduces
- **Less "bright"** — the pure chicken flavor gets muted under the murk

## What skimmed broth tastes like
- **Clean, round chicken flavor** front and center
- **Lighter on the tongue** even at the same fat content
- **Sweeter finish** — the natural savoriness comes through without interference
- **Holds up better** when reduced or used as a base for other dishes

## How noticeable is it really?

| Scenario | Detectability |
|---|---|
| Sipping broth straight | **Very noticeable** — side by side you'd pick the skimmed every time |
| Using as soup base with veggies/spices | **Moderately noticeable** — muddiness shows up in the background |
| Cooking rice or grains in it | **Mildly noticeable** — clean broth gives better grain flavor |
| Heavy sauce or stew with strong aromatics | **Barely noticeable** — garlic, herbs, tomato, etc. mask it |

## Bottom line for you
If you're drinking the broth for protein/collagen/minerals (which fits your stack), **skim it** — you'll actually enjoy sipping it instead of tolerating it. Takes 30 seconds, meaningful upgrade in drinkability. If it's just going into a heavily seasoned dish, don't stress it.
