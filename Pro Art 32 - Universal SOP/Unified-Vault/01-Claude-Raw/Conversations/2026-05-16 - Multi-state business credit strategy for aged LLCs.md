This is a general, educational overview of the relevant U.S. banking-law frameworks. It is not legal advice; the primary sources or a banking attorney should be consulted for any authoritative reading.

**Statutory and regulatory foundation**

The Truth in Lending Act (TILA, 15 U.S.C. § 1601 et seq.) is principally a consumer-protection and disclosure statute, implemented by Regulation Z (12 C.F.R. Part 1026). The Credit CARD Act of 2009 amended TILA to add a substantive underwriting obligation for credit card accounts. Under Regulation Z § 1026.51, a card issuer must, before opening an account or raising a credit limit, consider the consumer's ability to make the required payments, evaluated against the consumer's income or assets and current obligations. The regulation frames the income element as "current or reasonably expected income or assets."

One scoping point belongs at the outset: Regulation Z, including § 1026.51, governs consumer credit. Business-purpose credit is largely outside Regulation Z, so the ability-to-pay framework is properly understood as a consumer-credit doctrine, while commercial underwriting is governed mainly by safety-and-soundness supervision and the institution's own credit policy. Fair-lending law, discussed below, reaches further.

**1. Institutional underwriting benchmarks**

An underwriting benchmark is an instrument of the lender's internal credit policy. It is a risk-appetite threshold set by credit-risk management and ratified through institutional governance, typically a credit or risk committee and ultimately the board. Functionally, a benchmark is a decision rule the institution applies to the information before it, producing an output: approve or decline, an assigned limit, a pricing tier.

Legally, a benchmark is not a regulatory standard that an applicant "satisfies." It belongs to the lender and operates on the lender's side of the transaction. Its principal legal constraints are two: prudential supervision, under which examiners expect underwriting standards to be sound, documented, and consistently applied; and fair-lending law, which prohibits standards that produce unlawful disparate treatment or unjustified disparate impact.

**2. Applicant-specific projected income**

Projected income is an input, a forward-looking fact about a particular applicant. Regulation Z's "reasonably expected income" language accommodates it, but the modifier "reasonably expected" carries analytical weight. It functions as an issuer-side evidentiary standard: the issuer must hold a reasonable, articulable basis for the figure it relies upon, and that basis is necessarily individualized, grounded in the specific applicant's documented circumstances rather than a generic assumption.

Conceptually, projected income and an institutional benchmark sit on opposite sides of the transaction. The projection is an applicant-specific factual input; the benchmark is a lender-owned policy output. They are different objects, held by different parties, and one does not convert into or substitute for the other.

**3. Individualized underwriting requirements**

The unifying principle is that a creditworthiness or ability-to-pay determination must be individualized, tethered to the specific applicant rather than produced by formula in the abstract. Section 1026.51 requires that the issuer actually consider the particular consumer's ability to pay, and CFPB supervisory expectations treat that consideration as a substantive obligation rather than a formality. Benchmarks and scoring models are permitted tools, but they supplement rather than replace the individualized assessment, and they do not relax the requirement that the inputs feeding them be accurate.

**Supervisory architecture: CFPB and the prudential regulators**

Compliance is enforced through examination. The CFPB supervises larger depository institutions and specified nonbanks for federal consumer financial law, including TILA/Regulation Z and ECOA/Regulation B; the OCC, Federal Reserve, and FDIC supervise institutions within their respective charters for both consumer compliance and safety and soundness. Examiner methodology is broadly consistent across agencies: review of written policies and procedures, transaction testing through loan-file sampling, interviews, and, for fair lending, statistical analysis. The recurring examination questions are whether underwriting standards are sound and applied consistently, whether the ability-to-pay assessment genuinely occurred and was individualized, whether information is verified consistent with the institution's stated standards, and whether outcomes are free of prohibited discrimination.

**Model risk governance**

Where underwriting relies on quantitative models, the interagency model risk management guidance (Federal Reserve SR 11-7; OCC Bulletin 2011-12) applies. It rests on three broad pillars: disciplined model development, implementation, and use; independent model validation, which assesses conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring, and outcomes analysis; and governance, policies, and controls. A benchmark embedded in an underwriting model is part of that model's design and is subject to validation, but model governance does not alter the principle that a model must operate on accurate, applicant-specific data.

**Fair-lending frameworks**

The Equal Credit Opportunity Act (15 U.S.C. § 1691 et seq.), implemented by Regulation B (12 C.F.R. Part 1002), prohibits discrimination in any aspect of a credit transaction on prohibited bases, and notably applies to business credit as well as consumer credit. Liability is analyzed primarily under disparate-treatment and disparate-impact theories. A facially neutral benchmark can still draw scrutiny if it produces a disparate impact on a protected class that is not justified by business necessity. Examiners test for this through comparative file review and statistical methods.

**Synthesis**

The three concepts differ in both ownership and legal function. An institutional benchmark is a lender-owned policy instrument, evaluated under safety-and-soundness supervision and fair-lending law. Applicant-projected income is an applicant-specific factual input, legitimate under Regulation Z's "reasonably expected income" standard only insofar as it is individually substantiated. Individualized underwriting is the overarching requirement tying the assessment to the actual applicant: benchmarks and models are permissible instruments, but they neither displace the individualized determination nor change the requirement that the inputs be accurate and supportable.
