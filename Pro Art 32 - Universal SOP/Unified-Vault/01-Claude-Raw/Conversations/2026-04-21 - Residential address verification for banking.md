## Residence vs. Domicile — the actual law

**Domicile** = your one true, fixed, permanent home. You can only have one. Requires physical presence + intent to remain indefinitely.

**Residence** = where you actually live. You can have multiple. Requires physical presence, not necessarily permanence.

Both require **actual physical presence**. Neither is satisfied by a mailbox.

## What bank/credit applications actually ask

The language varies, but the common variants and what they legally mean:

| Application asks for | Legal meaning | Mailbox qualifies? |
|---|---|---|
| "Home address" | Where you actually live | No |
| "Residential address" | A place you actually reside | No |
| "Physical address" | Explicitly excludes PO boxes/CMRAs | No |
| "Current address" | Where you currently live | No |
| "Mailing address" | Where you receive mail | **Yes** (separate field) |

Most apps have **both fields** — residential *and* mailing — precisely because they're different. Putting a mail-forwarding address in the residential field is the misrepresentation.

## The federal rule that settles it

**31 CFR § 1020.220** (Customer Identification Program / Patriot Act) requires banks to collect:

> "A residential or business street address, or if the individual does not have such an address, an Army Post Office or Fleet Post Office box number, or the residential or business street address of next of kin or of another contact individual..."

Note what's **not** on the list: CMRAs, virtual mailboxes, mail forwarding services. The regulation explicitly contemplates that if you don't have a real street address, you use next of kin — not a mailbox service.

Banks operationalize this via the USPS CMRA database. If the address is registered as a CMRA, it fails the "residential street address" test automatically.

## Neither residence nor domicile helps the mailbox argument

- **Domicile** requires intent + presence → mailbox fails
- **Residence** requires actual living there → mailbox fails
- **CIP rule** requires a real street address, not a mail drop → mailbox fails

There's no legal word-game that makes a Florida mailbox into a valid answer to "residential address" when you actually live in Minnesota.

## Where the distinction *does* matter (legitimately)

The residence/domicile split is useful in these real scenarios:

1. **Snowbirds** — FL residence (condo 4 months/year) + NY domicile, or vice versa. Both real.
2. **Students** — residence in college town, domicile at parents' home.
3. **Military/diplomats** — stationed one place, domiciled another.
4. **Multi-state earners** — residence where you work, domicile where your permanent home is.

In each case, both addresses are **actual places the person physically occupies**. That's the thread.

## Scope of FL Stat. § 196.012

That definition lives in **Chapter 196 — Ad Valorem Tax Exemptions**. The chapter's own opening says those definitions apply "when used in this chapter." So § 196.012's "permanent residence" definition governs:

- Homestead exemption eligibility
- Senior/veteran/disability property tax exemptions
- Related ad valorem tax matters

It does **not** govern:
- Bank KYC / CIP requirements (those are federal — 31 CFR § 1020.220)
- Credit application "residential address" fields (bank underwriting standards)
- State income tax residency (FL has none, so moot)
- Federal tax residency (IRS rules, separate test)

So quoting 196.012 to a bank is a category error — it's the homestead exemption definition, not a banking definition.

## What 196.012 actually requires (read it carefully)

> "that place where a person has his or her **true, fixed, and permanent home** and **principal establishment** to which, whenever absent, he or she has the **intention of returning**"

Three elements, all required:

| Element | Meaning | Mailbox satisfies? |
|---|---|---|
| True, fixed, permanent home | An actual dwelling you occupy | No |
| Principal establishment | Your *main* base — not a secondary one | No |
| Intent to return when absent | You treat it as home, return to it | No |

This is **stricter** than generic "residence." It's essentially the legal definition of domicile. A place you've never slept fails all three prongs.

## The three methods — each requires real presence

You listed declaration of domicile, homestead, voter registration. Each one legally requires underlying actual residence:

- Sworn statement, filed with Clerk of Court
- Must state FL is your "predominant and principal home"

- False sworn statement = perjury (FL Stat. § 837.02, 3rd degree felony)

**Homestead Exemption — FL Const. Art. VII § 6 + FL Stat. § 196.031**
- Property must be your permanent residence per § 196.012
- Counties audit aggressively (they cross-check utility usage, driver's license, travel records)

**Voter Registration — FL Stat. § 97.041**

- False registration = 3rd degree felony (§ 104.011)

All three are **evidentiary markers** of domicile. None of them *creates* domicile where physical presence doesn't exist.
