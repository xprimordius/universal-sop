**Short answer: Usually no — but it depends on how the money is delivered and what the child does with it.**

**Key distinctions:**

**1. Inheritance is separate property by default.**
In all 50 states (including community property states), money a child inherits from a parent is considered the child's **separate property**, not marital property. A spouse has no automatic claim to it in a divorce.

**2. The #1 way that protection gets destroyed: commingling.**
If the child deposits the inheritance into a joint account, uses it to buy a jointly-titled house, pays down a joint mortgage, or otherwise mixes it with marital funds, courts can reclassify some or all of it as marital property. Once commingled, it's often impossible to untangle.

**3. The strongest protection: keep it IN the trust.**
Money distributed outright to the child = vulnerable to commingling, creditors, and divorce.
Money held in a properly drafted **spendthrift trust** with the child as beneficiary = generally protected. The spouse can't reach trust assets, and in most states even distributions for the child's benefit (housing, education, etc.) stay protected if structured right.

**4. If the child dies, different rules apply.**
The spouse may inherit through the child's will, intestacy, or **elective share** statutes (most states guarantee a surviving spouse some portion of the deceased spouse's estate — typically 30–50%). A continuing trust for descendants with the spouse excluded as beneficiary solves this.

---

**Practical takeaway for an estate plan:**
If the parent wants to make sure *only the kids* benefit — not in-laws — the plan should:
- Leave assets in a **continuing trust** for each child (not outright)
- Include **spendthrift** and **discretionary distribution** provisions
- Specify that descendants (grandkids) are remainder beneficiaries, not spouses
- Name an independent trustee

**Uncertainty flag:** Specific outcomes depend on (a) the state's marital property laws, (b) the exact trust language, (c) the child's own conduct with the funds, and (d) the jurisdiction of any divorce. This is general information, not legal advice — for an actual plan, an estate attorney in the relevant state needs to draft it.
