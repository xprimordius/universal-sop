Hey Alan. Short answer: **yes, hot soup in plastic absolutely leaches micro and nanoplastics**, even in "heat-resistant" containers. Here's the breakdown.

## Does It Leach?

A University of Nebraska study found that microwave heating caused the highest release of particles — some containers released up to 4.22 million microplastic and 2.11 billion nanoplastic particles per square centimeter in just 3 minutes of heating. Hot soup sitting in a plastic container isn't microwaving, but it's the same principle — heat weakens the chemical bonds in the plastic, allowing particles and chemicals to migrate into the food.

A 2025 study on polypropylene (PP) containers — one of the most common "heat-resistant" food plastics — confirmed nanoplastic release was significantly higher when rinsed with 90°C water vs room temperature.

Key point: "microwave safe" means the container won't melt — it does not mean it's safe for you, because heat still degrades the plastic and releases particles into food.

## Health Negatives

The research is still evolving from animal/cell models to confirmed human outcomes, but what's emerging is serious:

**Inflammation & oxidative stress** — in vivo exposure to micro/nanoplastics increases inflammatory markers like TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and NF-κB, driving inflammation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress.

**Endocrine disruption** — plastics contain endocrine-disrupting chemicals (BPA, phthalates, flame retardants) that bind to hormone receptors and disrupt function across thyroid, reproductive, and adrenal systems.

**Cardiovascular** — a March 2024 study found people with microplastics in their carotid artery tissue were twice as likely to have a heart attack, stroke, or die from any cause over the following three years.

**Immune suppression** — nanoplastics from food containers were readily absorbed by immune cells (macrophages) and significantly suppressed lysosomal activity, which is linked to impaired cellular cleanup and increased reactive oxygen species.

**Reproductive & neurological** — evidence suggests exposure may elevate risk of metabolic, respiratory, cardiovascular, neuroendocrine, hepatic, renal disorders, cancer, and aging-related conditions.

**Cell death** — particles extracted from plastic containers killed roughly 77% of human kidney cells at tested concentrations within 48-72 hours in vitro.

## The Move

For someone optimizing at your level — ditch plastic for hot food entirely. Glass, ceramic, or stainless steel. No exceptions for anything heated. The cumulative exposure compounds over time, and given your focus on inflammation management, hormonal optimization, and peak performance, this is a high-leverage low-effort swap.
