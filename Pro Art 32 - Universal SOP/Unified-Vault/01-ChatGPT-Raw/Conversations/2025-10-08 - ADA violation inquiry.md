## Applying to Your Scenario: Railing Forcing a Detour

Let me restate what you described:

> Coming from the north, you have to walk *past* the station entrance, then double back behind a railing to get to the station. But coming from the south, you can walk directly to the entrance without that detour.

Key questions to test whether this is an ADA violation:

1. **Is the railing creating a barrier in the only feasible route (or mainstream route)?**  
   If pedestrians (including people with disabilities) are *required* to take a longer, less direct route due to the railing, that suggests an obstacle.

2. **Is there an equally direct, accessible alternative for everyone?**  
   If the southern route (direct) is accessible, but the northern route is not, that disparity could be discriminatory.

3. **Was the railing part of new construction or alteration?**  
   If yes, ADA/Accessibility standards would require compliance when built. If no (older structure), there is more leeway unless removal is “readily achievable.”

4. **Is removal/modification of the railing “readily achievable” (i.e. easy/low cost) to fix the barrier?**  
   ADA (for public accommodations) uses the “readily achievable” standard for barrier removal in existing facilities. If it’s easy to fix, the entity may be obligated.  
   In transit / public entity settings, the stricter standard may apply.  

5. **Does the detour create an undue burden or fundamental alteration?**  
   If modifying or removing the railing would fundamentally alter the project or cost is disproportionate, a defendant may argue it's not required.

6. **Is the entity a public entity or private?**  
   Transit is generally a public entity subject under ADA Title II. That means stricter obligations.

Given those, your scenario **could** be a valid claim: the railing is creating an uneven burden depending on direction, effectively denying equally direct access. A court or regulatory body would examine how “accessible route” is defined in that context.

If this were mine to test:

- I would survey whether the northern approach is legally supposed to provide an accessible route equally.
- Check the engineering or planning documents: was there an accessible design alternative?
- Determine the cost and feasibility of reconfiguring the railing.

## Risks, Counterarguments & Realities

- The entity might argue the railing is necessary for safety (e.g. to prevent people from walking across tracks or traffic). That can justify certain design constraints.
- They might claim structural constraints or site topography make rerouting infeasible.
- If the railing was grandfathered (pre-existing) and no alteration triggered a requirement, they may not be forced retroactive compliance.
- The “readily achievable” defense may apply in many contexts, limiting liability for expensive fixes.
