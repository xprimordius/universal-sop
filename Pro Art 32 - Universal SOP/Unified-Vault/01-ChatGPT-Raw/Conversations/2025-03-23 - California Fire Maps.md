## 🤖 ChatGPT

Your observation reflects a common reaction to images from wildfire aftermaths where homes are completely destroyed, yet trees and vegetation nearby appear untouched. It does look unnatural—but there are scientifically valid reasons:

---

### **1. Material Composition & Combustibility**
- **Homes contain flammable materials**: synthetic fabrics, wood framing, insulation, propane, fuel lines, etc.
- **Wild trees contain moisture**: making them **less likely to ignite** unless exposed to direct, sustained heat.

---

### **2. Ember Storms (Firebrands)**
- Wildfires can send **embers flying miles ahead**, landing on rooftops, decks, or inside vents, igniting homes from within—**without needing flames to touch vegetation around**.
- This leads to **home-to-home ignition**, not necessarily trees.

---

### **3. Wind and Heat Channeling**
- Urban layouts **funnel wind and heat** between buildings—causing **temperature spikes** in tight clusters.
- Trees in open space **don’t trap heat the same way**.

---

### **4. Defensible Space and Fire Behavior**
- Some homes with **defensible space** survive; others with **overgrowth, mulch, or wood fencing** perish.
- The fire **selectively destroys based on layout, wind, and fuel continuity**, not at random.

---

### **5. Misleading Visuals**
- Burnt trees may still **retain green leaves for weeks** post-fire.
- Photos taken **early or at the right angle** may create the illusion they’re untouched.

---

### **Conclusion:**
It looks unnatural, but wildfire physics explain this pattern without requiring man-made causes. That said, many experts do acknowledge the role of **power lines, poor infrastructure, and human negligence**—which can **still be man-made causes**, but not deliberate ones (i.e., not DEWs or conspiracy-based).
