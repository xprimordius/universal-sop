While the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act requires only basic verification sufficient to confirm that a creditor claims a debt exists (15 U.S.C. §1692g), any attempt to legally enforce a contractual obligation requires proof of a valid and enforceable agreement.
Under established contract law principles, the party asserting the existence of a contract bears the burden of proving:
• mutual assent
• the specific contractual terms
• the consumer’s knowing acceptance of those terms
See Restatement (Second) of Contracts §17.
Accordingly, if you intend to continue attempting to collect this alleged obligation, you must produce documentation demonstrating:
The exact agreement presented to the consumer at the time of checkout, including the full text of the terms referenced in the interface.
Clear and conspicuous disclosure of any fixed-term commitment, automatic renewal, or early-termination liability at the point of purchase.
Verifiable records of affirmative acceptance of those terms, including timestamped electronic consent logs, IP records, and the exact version of the agreement in effect at the time of the transaction.
A complete accounting of the balance claimed, including invoices, fees, and the contractual basis for each charge.
Absent such documentation, continued attempts to collect payment based solely on internal billing records or unsupported assertions may constitute attempts to collect a debt without a valid contractual basis, and may implicate prohibitions against false, deceptive, or misleading representations in connection with the collection of a debt under 15 U.S.C. §1692e, as well as prohibitions against unfair or deceptive acts or practices under the Federal Trade Commission Act (15 U.S.C. §45).
Furthermore, enforceability of any alleged electronic agreement requires proof that the consumer affirmatively consented to the specific terms presented, consistent with the Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act (E-SIGN Act), 15 U.S.C. §7001, which provides legal effect to electronic agreements only where a consumer has knowingly assented to the terms of the electronic record.
Where material contractual terms - such as a multi-year financial commitment or accelerated liability for cancellation - are not clearly and conspicuously disclosed to the consumer prior to acceptance, courts routinely find a lack of mutual assent and decline to enforce those terms. Contract provisions that are hidden, inconspicuous, or materially inconsistent with the consumer-facing representation of the service may also constitute misrepresentation or deceptive trade practices.

Clear and Conspicuous Disclosure Standard
Under federal consumer protection law, material terms that could affect a consumer’s decision must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously at the time of the transaction. The Federal Trade Commission explains that disclosures must be presented in a manner that is difficult to miss and easily understandable by ordinary consumers, and they must appear before the consumer makes a purchasing decision, not after the transaction is completed or hidden within secondary documents or hyperlinks.
See FTC Guidance on Clear and Conspicuous Disclosures:
https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2014/09/full-disclosure
https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/attachments/press-releases/ftc-staff-issues-guidelines-internet-advertising/0005dotcomstaffreport.pdf

If the alleged fixed-term obligation or accelerated cancellation liability was not presented clearly in the purchase flow at the time of acceptance, then the consumer could not have provided knowing and informed assent to those terms. In such circumstances, courts frequently find that the disputed provision is unenforceable due to lack of mutual assent and inadequate disclosure of material terms.
