The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) and related U.S. Code sections do not explicitly state that an inquiry must be removed if you have not received anything (such as credit or services) as a result of that inquiry. However, there are provisions within the FCRA that could be relevant to challenging unauthorized or inaccurate inquiries.

### Relevant FCRA Sections:
1. **15 U.S.C. § 1681b(f) - Permissible Purposes of Consumer Reports**: This section outlines the permissible purposes for which a consumer report (credit report) can be accessed. If a credit inquiry was made without a permissible purpose, it could be considered unauthorized.

2. **15 U.S.C. § 1681i - Procedure in Case of Disputed Accuracy**: This section requires credit reporting agencies to investigate disputes raised by consumers. If you believe an inquiry on your report is inaccurate or unauthorized, you can dispute it with the credit bureau, and they must investigate the matter.

3. **15 U.S.C. § 1681n - Civil Liability for Willful Noncompliance**: This section addresses willful noncompliance with the FCRA, allowing for damages if a company knowingly violates your rights under the FCRA.

### Application:
If you notice an inquiry on your credit report that you did not authorize or did not result in receiving credit or services, you can dispute the inquiry with the credit bureaus. The argument could be that the inquiry was not made with a permissible purpose, and therefore, should be removed under the provisions mentioned above.

In summary, while there isn't a specific FCRA law or U.S. Code that directly states an inquiry must be removed if you haven't received anything in return, the relevant sections of the FCRA provide mechanisms to dispute unauthorized or inaccurate inquiries.

The Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) itself is not generally considered to violate constitutional rights. In fact, the FCRA was enacted to protect consumers' rights by ensuring fairness, accuracy, and privacy in the handling of their credit information. However, disputes can arise over whether certain applications of the FCRA or actions by credit reporting agencies, furnishers of information, or others might infringe on constitutional rights. These disputes typically involve issues related to due process, privacy, or equal protection under the law.

### Potential Constitutional Issues:
1. **Due Process (Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments):**
   - **What It Protects:** The Fifth Amendment (applies to the federal government) and the Fourteenth Amendment (applies to state actions) protect individuals from being deprived of "life, liberty, or property, without due process of law."
   - **FCRA Context:** If a credit reporting agency or other entity provides inaccurate information that leads to a negative credit report without giving the consumer a fair opportunity to dispute or correct that information, there could be an argument that this violates due process rights. However, the FCRA includes provisions that allow consumers to dispute inaccuracies, which generally addresses this concern.

2. **Privacy (Implied Rights from Various Amendments):**
   - **What It Protects:** The right to privacy is implied from various amendments in the Constitution, including the First, Fourth, and Fourteenth Amendments.
   - **FCRA Context:** The FCRA regulates how personal financial information can be collected, shared, and used. Some concerns about privacy might arise if a consumer believes their personal information is being used improperly or without their consent. However, the FCRA is designed to protect consumer privacy, and violations of the FCRA's provisions can lead to legal action against those who misuse personal information.

3. **Equal Protection (Fourteenth Amendment):**
   - **What It Protects:** The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires that individuals in similar situations be treated equally by the law.
   - **FCRA Context:** If a consumer believes they are being treated unfairly based on race, gender, or another protected characteristic in the context of credit reporting or lending, they might raise an equal protection claim. However, this would typically involve specific actions by a private or governmental entity rather than a claim that the FCRA itself is unconstitutional.

### Conclusion:
The FCRA was designed to protect consumer rights and is not generally viewed as violating constitutional rights. However, if the FCRA is applied in a way that leads to unfair treatment, deprivation of due process, or invasion of privacy, a consumer could potentially argue that their constitutional rights have been infringed. Such cases would be highly fact-specific and would generally be addressed through the court system.
