Unconscionability in contract law refers to terms that are so unjust or overwhelmingly one-sided in favor of the party who has the superior bargaining power that they are deemed unenforceable. The concept is applied through both state and federal laws in the U.S. Here are some of the key legal sources:

### 1. **Uniform Commercial Code (UCC) - Section 2-302**
   - **UCC § 2-302** deals with the concept of unconscionable contracts or clauses, particularly in the sale of goods.
   - **Key Provision:**
     - The court may refuse to enforce the contract, enforce the remainder of the contract without the unconscionable clause, or limit the application of the unconscionable clause to avoid an unconscionable result.
   - **Text:**
     - "If the court as a matter of law finds the contract or any clause of the contract to have been unconscionable at the time it was made the court may refuse to enforce the contract, or it may enforce the remainder of the contract without the unconscionable clause, or it may so limit the application of any unconscionable clause as to avoid any unconscionable result."

### 2. **Restatement (Second) of Contracts - Section 208**
   - **Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 208** is a general guide for courts on how to handle unconscionable contracts.
   - **Key Provision:**
     - A contract or term may be deemed unenforceable if it is found to be unconscionable at the time the contract was made.
   - **Text:**
     - "If a contract or term thereof is unconscionable at the time the contract is made a court may refuse to enforce the contract, or may enforce the remainder of the contract without the unconscionable term, or may so limit the application of any unconscionable term as to avoid any unconscionable result."

### 3. **Federal Arbitration Act (FAA) - 9 U.S.C. § 2**
   - While the FAA does not explicitly address unconscionability, the U.S. Supreme Court has acknowledged that unconscionability can be a ground for invalidating arbitration agreements.
   - **Key Case Law:**
     - *AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion*, 563 U.S. 333 (2011): The Supreme Court held that arbitration agreements are generally enforceable under the FAA, but state law defenses such as unconscionability still apply.

### 4. **State Laws**
   - Many states have their own statutes or case law dealing with unconscionability. For example:
     - **California Civil Code § 1670.5**:
       - California codified the principle of unconscionability, allowing courts to refuse to enforce all or part of a contract found to be unconscionable.
       - **Text:**
         - "If the court as a matter of law finds the contract or any clause of the contract to have been unconscionable at the time it was made, the court may refuse to enforce the contract, or it may enforce the remainder of the contract without the unconscionable clause, or it may so limit the application of any unconscionable clause as to avoid any unconscionable result."
     - **New York General Obligations Law § 5-701**:
       - New York law also recognizes unconscionability, particularly in consumer transactions.

### 5. **Case Law on Unconscionability**
   - **Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co., 350 F.2d 445 (D.C. Cir. 1965)**:
     - This case is a landmark decision where the court established the modern doctrine of unconscionability, focusing on both procedural and substantive aspects.
   - **Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc., 24 Cal. 4th 83 (2000)**:
     - The California Supreme Court provided a detailed analysis of unconscionability, particularly in the context of arbitration agreements.

### Key Concepts of Unconscionability:
   - **Procedural Unconscionability**: Relates to the manner in which the contract was negotiated and the circumstances of the parties at the time, including issues like inequality of bargaining power or hidden terms.
   - **Substantive Unconscionability**: Refers to the actual terms of the contract being overly harsh or one-sided.

### Practical Application:
   - Courts typically use a sliding scale where a contract or clause may be deemed unenforceable if there is a sufficient degree of either procedural or substantive unconscionability.

### Summary
Unconscionability is a doctrine that allows courts to refuse to enforce contracts or terms that are deemed unjust or excessively one-sided. Key legal sources include UCC § 2-302, Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 208, various state laws, and case law like *Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co.* and *Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc.*

For specific cases or applications, consult state laws or case law applicable in your jurisdiction, as interpretations and enforcement can vary.

### 1. **California Civil Code § 1668: Contracts Exempting a Party from Responsibility**

**Statutory Text:**

**Exact Wording:**

**Exact Wording:**

(b) Grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement."

### 3. **Notable Case Law:**

#### **A. *Discover Bank v. Superior Court (Boehr), 36 Cal. 4th 148 (2005)***

- **Key Points:**
  - The California Supreme Court in *Discover Bank* addressed the issue of class action waivers in arbitration agreements. The court found that in certain situations, such waivers can be deemed unconscionable, particularly when they are part of contracts of adhesion (take-it-or-leave-it contracts) and involve small amounts of damages that make individual actions impractical.
  - This case established the "Discover Bank Rule," which allowed courts to declare class action waivers in arbitration agreements unconscionable if they were found to be oppressive and to effectively exculpate the drafting party from liability.

- **Exact Wording:**
  - "We do not hold that all class action waivers are necessarily unconscionable. But when the waiver is found in a consumer contract of adhesion in a setting in which disputes between the contracting parties predictably involve small amounts of damages, and when it is alleged that the party with the superior bargaining power has carried out a scheme to deliberately cheat large numbers of consumers out of individually small sums of money, then, at least to the extent the obligation at issue is governed by California law, the waiver becomes in practice the exemption of the party ‘from responsibility for [its] own fraud, or willful injury to the person or property of another.’ Under these circumstances, such waivers are unconscionable under California law and should not be enforced."

- **Impact:**
  - The *Discover Bank* ruling was significant in allowing challenges to class action waivers in arbitration agreements on the grounds of unconscionability.

#### **B. *AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion, 563 U.S. 333 (2011)***

- **Key Points:**
  - In *Concepcion*, the U.S. Supreme Court overturned the *Discover Bank* rule, holding that the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA) preempts state laws that categorically prohibit contracts from disallowing class-wide arbitration. The Court ruled that class action waivers in arbitration agreements are enforceable, even if the underlying state law would deem them unconscionable.
  - The decision emphasized that the FAA's purpose is to promote arbitration and that allowing states to invalidate arbitration agreements based on unconscionability principles that disfavor arbitration would undermine this purpose.

- **Exact Wording:**
  - "The overarching purpose of the FAA, evident in the text of §§ 2, 3, and 4, is to ensure the enforcement of arbitration agreements according to their terms so as to facilitate streamlined proceedings."

- **Impact:**
  - *Concepcion* significantly limited the ability of plaintiffs to use state unconscionability laws to invalidate class action waivers in arbitration agreements, strengthening the enforceability of such waivers.

### 4. **Indemnification Clauses and Unconscionability**

#### **A. *Ingle v. Circuit City Stores, Inc., 328 F.3d 1165 (9th Cir. 2003)***

- **Key Points:**
  - In *Ingle*, the Ninth Circuit addressed an arbitration agreement that included a class action waiver and an indemnification clause requiring the employee to pay the employer’s legal fees in certain circumstances. The court found these provisions unconscionable.
  - The court ruled that the indemnification clause was one-sided and unduly oppressive, rendering it substantively unconscionable. Combined with other factors, such as the employer's superior bargaining power and the non-negotiable nature of the contract, the court held that the arbitration agreement as a whole was unconscionable.

- **Exact Wording:**
  - "We conclude that the fee-splitting provision, which obligates employees to split arbitration costs with Circuit City regardless of their financial ability, is unconscionable under California law because it precludes employees from vindicating their statutory rights."

- **Impact:**
  - *Ingle* is an important case for understanding how courts may view indemnification clauses in the context of arbitration agreements, especially when combined with class action waivers and other provisions that disadvantage the weaker party.

#### **B. *Hooters of America, Inc. v. Phillips, 173 F.3d 933 (4th Cir. 1999)***

- **Key Points:**
  - In this case, the Fourth Circuit found an arbitration agreement unconscionable because of the inclusion of numerous provisions that heavily favored the employer, including an indemnification clause that would require the employee to pay significant costs if they lost the arbitration.
  - The court concluded that the arbitration process designed by Hooters was so one-sided that it was not a real arbitration process and was therefore unconscionable.

- **Exact Wording:**
  - "Although we are mindful of the strong federal policy favoring arbitration, we refuse to enforce an arbitration agreement that is so one-sided that it is unconscionable."

- **Impact:**
  - This case demonstrates that courts can find arbitration agreements unenforceable if they include provisions that are excessively one-sided, such as indemnification clauses that unfairly burden the weaker party.

### Summary
These statutes and cases demonstrate how courts approach the intersection of unconscionability with class action waivers and indemnification clauses in contracts:

- **California Civil Code § 1668** and **§ 1670.5** are key statutory provisions that prohibit contracts from exempting a party from liability for fraud or other unlawful acts and that allow courts to invalidate unconscionable contract clauses.
- **Discover Bank v. Superior Court** established a precedent for finding class action waivers unconscionable under certain conditions, though this was later preempted by the FAA in *AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion*.
- Cases like *Ingle v. Circuit City* and *Hooters of America v. Phillips* show how courts may find indemnification clauses in arbitration agreements unconscionable, especially when combined with other unfair provisions.

These legal principles form the basis for challenging unconscionable class action waivers and indemnification clauses in various jurisdictions.
