- **Long-Term Impact:** Over time, the negative impact of a discharge lessens, especially if it wasn't through bankruptcy. However, it could still be a red flag for some lenders when considering future credit applications.

**Credit Score Consideration:**
- If the discharge is due to reasons like disability or forgiveness programs, it might not have as severe an impact as bankruptcy discharge.
- The discharge could lead to a short-term dip in your credit score, but it might improve your score in the long term by reducing your overall debt load.

### 2. **Student Loans Open with Balance and 0% Paid Off**

**Impact on Credit Report:**
- **Negative Aspect:** An open loan with a 0% payment history is generally seen as a significant negative factor. It indicates to lenders that you are either unable or unwilling to repay the loan, which raises concerns about your creditworthiness.
- **Ongoing Impact:** This could continue to drag down your credit score, as the account is reported as delinquent or in non-payment status.
- **Credit Utilization:** Keeping a high balance on loans without making payments negatively impacts your credit utilization ratio, further hurting your credit score.

**Credit Score Consideration:**
- Having a large, unpaid balance with no payment history is one of the worst scenarios for your credit score. It will significantly lower your score, and the longer it remains unpaid, the worse the impact.
- The non-payment status might also result in collection actions, further harming your credit report.

### **Which Is Worse?**

- **Discharged Loans**: Generally speaking, having student loans discharged is less damaging in the long term than having open loans with 0% paid off. - **Open Loans with 0% Paid Off**: This scenario is worse because it continuously harms your credit score and indicates ongoing financial distress or irresponsibility. It also opens you up to potential collection actions, which can lead to additional negative marks on your credit report.

> ## Are the Upgrade cards actually credit cards?
> 
> Not really. An Upgrade card looks like a credit card, and since you can use it in stores, it acts like a credit card. But it’s actually considered a “series of closed-end loans,'' according to the terms and conditions. Any time you make a purchase or request that funds be transferred to your bank account, you’re borrowing against your credit limit (which can range from $500 to $25,000).
> 
> You can borrow from that limit multiple times simultaneously. And instead of making at least a minimum payment when the bill is due, you’re making payments in equal installments like you would with a 【264†personal loan】.
