## 🤖 ChatGPT

A decrease in your credit score after paying down your credit card balance could be due to several factors, despite the positive action of lowering your utilization. Here are some possibilities:

1. **Account Information Change**: The screenshot mentions that a remark code was added to your MISSION LANE TAB BAN account. Depending on the nature of this remark, it could have negatively impacted your credit score. Remarks like disputes or adverse actions can affect your score.

2. **Credit Utilization Changes**: While lowering your balance is generally good, if your overall credit utilization across all cards or specific timing of when balances were reported changed, it might have caused a temporary score fluctuation. If you had recently paid off another card, and the timing of reporting led to a higher balance being reported overall, this could impact your score.

3. **Recent Credit Inquiries or New Accounts**: If you recently applied for new credit or opened a new account, the inquiry or the new credit line might have temporarily lowered your score.

4. **Age of Accounts**: If any accounts were closed or the average age of your accounts decreased due to a new account being added, this could impact your score negatively.

5. **Credit Mix and Derogatory Marks**: If there were changes in your credit mix (e.g., closing a type of credit that diversifies your profile) or any new derogatory marks were added, your score could drop.

Review any recent activity on your credit report, especially the specific remark mentioned, as it could provide insight into why the score dropped after what should have been a positive action.

---

## 🤖 ChatGPT

When you see an increase in your credit score after increasing your credit card debt, it can seem counterintuitive, but a few factors may explain this:

1. **Credit Utilization Ratio**: 
   - Your credit utilization ratio is a significant factor in your credit score. However, it's not just about keeping it low but also about managing it within a specific range that credit scoring models may favor. For example, if your credit utilization was extremely low (e.g., below 1%), adding some debt can actually improve your score by showing that you're using your available credit responsibly.

2. **Increased Activity**:
   - Credit scoring models may also favor a certain level of activity on your credit accounts. If you had very little usage on your credit card, then adding a modest amount of debt could indicate to the scoring models that you are actively and responsibly using your credit, leading to a score increase.

3. **Credit Mix and Payment History**:
   - Adding a small amount of debt, especially if you maintain a perfect payment history, could positively impact your score by improving the diversity of your credit mix and showing active management of revolving credit.

4. **Authorized User Impact**:
   - As an authorized user, your score could be influenced by the primary account holder's credit usage and payment history. A slight increase in utilization (as long as it's not excessive) might reflect positively depending on how the credit scoring model assesses shared credit responsibilities.

5. **Threshold Effects**:
   - Credit scoring models often use thresholds, and adding debt might have moved you into a more favorable range. For example, moving from 1% to 5% utilization might positively impact your score because you're perceived as responsibly using your credit without overextending.

In summary, your score might increase due to better utilization ratios, more active credit usage, or other factors in the scoring algorithm that favor responsible credit behavior, even if it involves a modest amount of debt.
