# Impact of Closed Credit

---

## 👤 You

Will carrying a 5% balance on a closed credit card account negatively affect my credit and my ability to attain credit? 

Or am I better off just paying it off completely? 


Any difference?

---

## 🤖 ChatGPT

Carrying a 5% balance on a closed credit card account can indeed have a negative impact on your credit and your ability to attain new credit. Here’s why:

1. **Credit Utilization:** Even though the account is closed, the balance still affects your credit utilization ratio, which is the amount of credit you're using compared to your total available credit. A lower credit utilization ratio is generally better for your credit score. By keeping a balance, you increase this ratio, potentially lowering your score.

2. **Interest and Fees:** Carrying a balance may result in interest charges and potentially late fees if you're not careful about making payments. These could further negatively impact your credit.

3. **Debt-to-Income Ratio:** Lenders often consider your debt-to-income ratio when deciding whether to extend new credit. A balance, even a small one, could affect this ratio and reduce your chances of approval for new credit.

4. **Perception of Risk:** Lenders may view any unpaid balance, particularly on a closed account, as a sign of financial instability or risk. This could make them more hesitant to offer you new credit or might result in less favorable terms.

**Conclusion:**
It’s generally better to pay off the balance completely. Doing so will:
- Reduce your credit utilization ratio.
- Eliminate any potential interest or fees.
- Improve your overall credit profile and likelihood of getting new credit at better terms.

In summary, paying off the balance is the optimal move for maintaining or improving your credit score and increasing your chances of attaining new credit.

## 👤 You

Can paid/closed never late still be a charge off internally

---

## 🤖 ChatGPT

Yes, an account can be considered an "internal charge-off" by a creditor even if it's marked as "paid" or "closed" and shows no late payments on your credit report. Here's how this can happen:

1. **Internal Charge-Off:** Sometimes, a creditor might internally charge off an account due to various reasons, such as prolonged delinquency, operational errors, or changes in the status of the account that do not immediately reflect in the credit reporting system. This internal designation is used by the creditor for their accounting purposes, meaning they have written off the debt as uncollectible and moved it to a different category in their books.

2. **Credit Reporting:** Even though the account is internally charged off, it may not reflect as a charge-off on your credit report if you were never late on payments and eventually paid the balance in full. Instead, it might be reported as "paid," "closed," or "settled," without indicating any negative marks like late payments or charge-offs.

3. **Impact on Future Credit:** While this internal designation might not show up on your credit report, it could still affect your relationship with the creditor. They may use this information in their internal risk assessments, which could influence future credit decisions with that particular creditor or related entities.

**Key Points:**
- **Credit Report:** As long as the account shows as paid/closed and never late, your credit report remains clean from any charge-off notation.
- **Internal Records:** The internal charge-off might not impact your credit score directly but could affect how that creditor views your creditworthiness in the future.

In summary, while a paid/closed account with no late payments may not show a charge-off on your credit report, it could still be classified as a charge-off internally by the creditor, potentially influencing future dealings with them.

## 👤 You

How to remove internal charge off data 

And to discover it

---

## 🤖 ChatGPT

Removing internal charge-off data and discovering if it exists can be challenging, as this information is typically not accessible to consumers directly and isn't reported on your credit report. Here’s how you can approach both discovering and potentially addressing an internal charge-off:

### Discovering an Internal Charge-Off

1. **Request Your Account History:** Contact the creditor directly and request a detailed account history or payment ledger. This document may reveal if the account was internally classified as a charge-off at any point.

2. **Communicate with the Creditor:** Speak with a customer service representative or, better yet, a supervisor or someone in the credit risk or collections department. Ask if there’s any internal charge-off status or any derogatory notes associated with your account.

3. **Check with Credit Bureaus:** Although internal charge-offs may not appear on your credit report, sometimes creditors may report certain details that could give you a clue, such as a "settled" status instead of "paid in full." Request your detailed credit report from all three major bureaus (Experian, TransUnion, and Equifax) to look for any subtle hints.

### Removing Internal Charge-Off Data

1. **Negotiate with the Creditor:**
   - **Request Reconsideration:** If you discover that there’s an internal charge-off, you can request the creditor to reconsider this classification, especially if there were extenuating circumstances that led to the charge-off.
   - **Goodwill Adjustment:** If your payment history is generally positive, you can ask for a goodwill adjustment. This is a request for the creditor to remove any negative internal notes as a gesture of goodwill, especially if you’ve resolved the outstanding balance.

2. **Provide Evidence:** If you believe the internal charge-off is unjustified, provide documentation showing your payments were timely, and the account was paid off. This could strengthen your case for removal.

3. **Escalate the Issue:** If the creditor is uncooperative, consider escalating the issue to a higher authority within the company, such as the Office of the President or a special customer service resolution team.

4. **Legal Action:** In rare cases, if the internal charge-off is causing significant damage (e.g., denial of new credit or higher interest rates), you might consider consulting with an attorney who specializes in credit or consumer rights to explore further options.

### Key Points to Remember
- **Documentation is Crucial:** Keep all correspondence and documents related to your interactions with the creditor.
- **Be Persistent:** Dealing with internal records can be a slow process, and it may take multiple attempts to get the desired outcome.
- **Future Credit Relationships:** Even if you can’t remove the internal charge-off, ensure you maintain good standing with other creditors to minimize the impact.

Ultimately, removing internal charge-off data relies heavily on your ability to negotiate and communicate effectively with the creditor. While it's not always possible to remove, persistence and a well-documented case can often lead to a positive resolution.

## 👤 You

Do I have the right to this information upon request?

---

## 🤖 ChatGPT

Generally, you do not have an automatic right to access internal charge-off information or other internal records held by creditors. Here's why:

### Understanding Your Rights:
1. **Credit Reporting Rights:** Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), you have the right to access your credit report from the major credit bureaus (Experian, Equifax, and TransUnion) and dispute any inaccuracies you find. However, this does not extend to internal records that creditors keep for their own purposes.

2. **Internal Records:** Internal charge-offs and related notes are part of the creditor’s internal accounting and risk management processes. These are typically not disclosed to consumers because they are considered proprietary information used by the creditor for their own business purposes.

3. **Access to Account Information:** While you don’t have a legal right to demand internal charge-off records, you do have the right to request detailed information about your account history, such as payment records, balances, and account statuses, under general consumer protection laws. Whether the creditor chooses to disclose any internal designations (such as charge-offs) when providing this information is at their discretion.

### How to Request Information:
1. **Contact the Creditor:** Politely request a detailed account history, including any notes or internal classifications related to the account. You can emphasize your desire to understand how the account was managed internally.

2. **Use Specific Language:** When communicating with the creditor, it might be helpful to ask directly if the account was ever subject to an "internal charge-off" or similar internal designation. You can also ask if there are any internal records that might affect your future creditworthiness.

3. **Leverage Your Consumer Rights:** While you may not have rights to internal records, if you suspect that an internal charge-off has negatively impacted your credit or relationship with the creditor, you can raise the issue under general principles of fairness or as a dispute regarding how your account was managed.

4. **Persistence:** If you are initially denied access to the information, you can request to speak with a supervisor or escalate the matter to a higher level within the company. Persistence and a clear, respectful request often help.

### Final Notes:
- **Consult Legal Advice:** If the internal charge-off has caused significant harm (e.g., denial of credit or higher interest rates) and the creditor refuses to provide information, you may consider consulting with an attorney specializing in consumer rights. They can advise you on any potential legal avenues.
- **Business Relations:** Keep in mind that while you may not have a legal right to this information, maintaining a positive relationship with the creditor could lead to them being more forthcoming with the information you seek.

In summary, while you don’t have an explicit legal right to access internal charge-off information, you can request detailed account information, and some creditors might be willing to share more if approached in the right manner.
