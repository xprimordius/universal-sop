---

# 1. If the Promise Was in the Written Contract
**Strongest protection.**

If the purchase agreement, warranty addendum, or service plan states oil changes are included:

- The dealer **must honor it**.
- Refusal is **breach of contract**.

Possible remedies:
- Demand the dealer perform the service.
- Reimbursement for paid services.
- Complaint to the state regulator.

Legal basis:
- Contract law
- Warranty enforcement

---

Still potentially enforceable.

Most states prohibit **deceptive or misleading sales practices**.

- **Misrepresentation**
- **Deceptive trade practice**

Relevant laws typically include:

| Law | Purpose |
|---|---|
| State Unfair and Deceptive Acts and Practices (UDAP) laws | Prohibit misleading sales tactics |
| FTC Act §5 | Prohibits unfair or deceptive acts in commerce |

Source:  
https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statutes/federal-trade-commission-act

Evidence that helps:
- text messages
- emails
- advertisements
- salesperson notes
- witnesses

“Free oil changes with CPO purchase”

## Short Answer  
If the promise was **only verbal and cannot be proven**, the buyer’s legal protection is **limited**, but not zero under California law.

The key issue becomes **evidence of misrepresentation**.

---

# 1. Contract Rule (Major Limitation)
Most auto purchase contracts include an **integration clause** stating:

> “This contract contains the entire agreement between the parties.”

When this exists, **verbal statements not written in the contract are generally not enforceable.**

Courts call this the **Parol Evidence Rule**.

Source:  
California Civil Code §1625  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=1625&lawCode=CIV

---

# 2. Exception — Fraud / Misrepresentation
Even with an integration clause, California law still allows claims for **fraud or deceptive practices**.

Relevant statute:

**California Civil Code §1710 – Deceit**  
Defines misrepresentation as:

> “The suggestion, as a fact, of that which is not true by one who does not believe it to be true.”

Source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=1710&lawCode=CIV

If a salesperson knowingly misrepresented the service coverage to induce the purchase, it can qualify as **fraud or deceit**.

---

# 3. California Consumer Protection Law
The strongest consumer protection statute is:

**California Unfair Competition Law (UCL)**  
Business & Professions Code §17200

It prohibits:

- unlawful business practices
- unfair business practices
- deceptive business practices

Source:  
https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=17200&lawCode=BPC

However, **proof is still required**.

---

# 4. Evidence That Courts Accept
Even if the promise was verbal, courts often accept **circumstantial evidence** such as:

- dealership advertising
- salesperson emails or texts
- service appointment notes
- witnesses present during purchase
- dealership patterns (other buyers received the same promise)

---

# 5. Practical Reality (Important)
If there is **no documentation or witnesses**, pursuing a legal claim over oil changes is usually **not economically practical**.

Dealers often resolve small disputes to avoid complaints.

---

# Bottom Line
If the promise was **purely verbal and unprovable**:

| Factor | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Written proof | Strong case |
| Witness / circumstantial evidence | Moderate case |
| No evidence | Weak case |

But California law **still prohibits deceptive sales practices**, even if proving them may be difficult.

# Legal Elements That Must Be Proven
California courts require **five elements** for fraud/misrepresentation.

Source: **Lazar v. Superior Court, 12 Cal.4th 631 (1996)**  
https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/4th/12/631.html

A plaintiff must prove:

| Element | What it Means |
|---|---|
| 1. Misrepresentation | A false statement or promise was made |
| 2. Knowledge of falsity | The person knew it was false or made it recklessly |
| 3. Intent to induce reliance | It was said to persuade the buyer to act |
| 4. Justifiable reliance | The buyer reasonably relied on it |
| 5. Damages | The buyer suffered harm because of it |

All **five must be proven**.

---

# What Counts as Proof
Proof does **not have to be a recording**, but there must be **evidence supporting the claim**.

Examples courts accept:

- Emails or text messages  
- Advertising or dealership promotions  
- Written notes from the transaction  
- Witness testimony  
- Pattern of the dealership offering the same promise to buyers

Even **circumstantial evidence** can be used.

---

# If There Is Truly No Evidence
If the situation is:

- verbal statement only  
- no witnesses  
- nothing written  
- nothing advertised  

Then **proving misrepresentation becomes extremely difficult**.

Courts generally rely on **evidence, not memory alone**.
