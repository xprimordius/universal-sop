## Why Waymo “got there first” (or at least ahead in commercial deployment)

- They were allowed to **operate under special permits / regulatory frameworks** in specific cities (e.g. San Francisco, Los Angeles). citeturn0search5turn0search27turn0search20  
- They used **geofencing / limited zones** where their system is reliable. It’s easier to certify autonomy in constrained, mapped environments.  
- They avoided as much consumer risk exposure — as a service, not a sold feature.  
- Their safety record (or at least their risk management and data) has been strong enough to persuade regulators to grant them more leeway — and some investigations into Waymo have closed without action. citeturn0news51  

---

## Why Tesla hasn’t (yet) fully deployed FSD in the same way

- Tesla’s FSD is not fully autonomous — it’s still “always supervised” legally and technically. citeturn0search8turn0search2turn0search42  
- Regulators are wary because of Tesla’s crash reports, red-light running, etc. that have drawn scrutiny. citeturn0search11turn0search12turn0search24  
- Tesla hasn’t (yet) secured all the permits for fully driverless operation in many states. citeturn0search39turn0search12turn0search3  
- Consumer deployment vs fleet deployment: Waymo’s model is fleet-based and controlled; Tesla’s is distributed to consumers on variable road conditions.

## Bottom line

- “Allowed FSD” is not a monolithic thing — Waymo’s deployment is narrower, more regulated, and under special permits.
- Tesla’s FSD is broader in ambition, more exposed, and therefore faces stricter scrutiny.
- As regulation evolves (federal frameworks, state laws), Tesla could catch up — but the path is harder because of liability, safety, public trust, and existing incident history.
