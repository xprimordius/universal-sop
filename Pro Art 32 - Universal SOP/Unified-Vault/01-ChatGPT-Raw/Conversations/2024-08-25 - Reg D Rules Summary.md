### Regulation D: Rules 504 and 506

**Regulation D** under the Securities Act of 1933 provides exemptions from the registration requirements for certain securities offerings, allowing companies to raise capital without having to register their securities with the SEC. 

1. **Rule 504 (Reg D):**
   - **Scope:** Allows companies to offer and sell up to $10 million of securities in a 12-month period without registering with the SEC.
   - **Eligibility:** Generally used by smaller companies. The securities may be sold to an unlimited number of investors, and there are no restrictions on resale (with some exceptions).
   - **Restrictions:** Does not permit general solicitation or advertising unless the offering is conducted in compliance with state securities laws.

2. **Rule 506 (Reg D):**
   - **Scope:** Divided into two parts: 506(b) and 506(c).
     - **506(b):** Allows companies to raise an unlimited amount of money. They can sell securities to an unlimited number of accredited investors and up to 35 non-accredited investors who have sufficient knowledge and experience to evaluate the investment. General solicitation is not allowed.
     - **506(c):** Also allows companies to raise an unlimited amount, but all purchasers must be accredited investors, and the issuer must take reasonable steps to verify their accredited status. General solicitation is permitted.

### Tier 1 and Tier 2: Regulation A+

Regulation A+ (often referred to as a "mini-IPO") allows companies to offer and sell securities to the general public without having to register with the SEC, though there are two tiers with different requirements:

1. **Tier 1:**
   - **Scope:** Allows offerings of up to $20 million in a 12-month period.
   - **Regulation:** Subject to both state and federal review.
   - **Reporting:** Companies are not required to file ongoing reports with the SEC after the offering is completed, although they must provide an exit report.

2. **Tier 2:**
   - **Scope:** Allows offerings of up to $75 million in a 12-month period.
   - **Regulation:** Exempt from state securities law requirements (federal law preempts state law), but subject to more rigorous federal review.
   - **Reporting:** Companies must provide audited financial statements and file ongoing reports with the SEC (similar to those required of publicly traded companies).

### Difference Between a Board Member and an Officer

**Board Member:**
- **Role:** A board member (or director) is part of the board of directors, the governing body responsible for overseeing the company's overall strategy, policy decisions, and the hiring of executive management. The board provides high-level guidance and holds the management accountable.
- **Duties:** Includes setting the vision and direction for the company, making major decisions (such as mergers, acquisitions, or significant investments), and ensuring the company's compliance with laws and regulations.
- **Authority:** Board members do not handle the day-to-day operations but have the authority to appoint or remove officers.

**Officer:**
- **Role:** An officer is an executive, such as a CEO, CFO, or COO, responsible for the day-to-day operations of the company. Officers execute the strategic direction set by the board of directors.
- **Duties:** Involves managing the company’s daily operations, making operational decisions, and ensuring the company's strategy is implemented effectively.
- **Authority:** Officers have direct control over company operations and report to the board of directors. They often hold specific titles and responsibilities within the organization.

In summary:
- **Board members** are involved in oversight and governance, setting the long-term strategy.
- **Officers** are involved in the execution and management of daily operations, implementing the board's strategy.
