# Milgram Experiment Overview

---

## 👤 You

Milligram experiment with lethal voltage shock for learning

---

## 🤖 ChatGPT

You're likely referring to the **Milgram experiment**, a famous psychological study conducted by Yale University psychologist Stanley Milgram in the early 1960s. The experiment sought to explore the extent to which individuals would obey authority figures when instructed to perform acts that conflicted with their personal conscience, specifically in the context of administering what they believed to be painful and potentially lethal electric shocks to another person.

### Overview of the Milgram Experiment:

1. **Objective**:
   - The study aimed to investigate obedience to authority, particularly how far people would go in obeying instructions if it meant harming another individual. Milgram was interested in understanding the behavior of people during World War II, particularly how ordinary Germans participated in or enabled the atrocities of the Holocaust.

2. **Methodology**:
   - **Participants**: The experiment involved 40 male participants, who were told they were taking part in a study on learning and memory.
   - **Roles**: The participants were assigned the role of the "teacher," while an actor played the "learner." The learner was placed in another room and was strapped to a chair with electrodes attached to their arms.
   - **Procedure**: The teacher was instructed by the experimenter (authority figure) to administer electric shocks to the learner whenever they answered a question incorrectly. The shocks were to increase in voltage with each incorrect answer, ranging from 15 volts to 450 volts.
   - **Apparatus**: The shock generator had labels ranging from "Slight Shock" to "Danger: Severe Shock" and, at the highest level, "XXX," suggesting extreme danger.
   - **Obedience Pressure**: As the shocks increased, the learner (who was actually an actor and not receiving real shocks) would begin to scream in pain, beg for the experiment to stop, and eventually fall silent, simulating unconsciousness or death. The experimenter would pressure the teacher to continue, using prompts such as "The experiment requires that you continue," "It is absolutely essential that you continue," and "You have no other choice; you must go on."

3. **Results**:
   - A significant majority of the participants (65%) continued to administer shocks up to the maximum 450-volt level, despite the apparent distress of the learner.
   - Many participants were visibly distressed, sweating, trembling, and showing signs of extreme stress, but they continued to obey the experimenter's instructions.

4. **Conclusion**:
   - The Milgram experiment demonstrated the powerful influence of authority on human behavior. It revealed that ordinary people are capable of committing harmful actions when instructed by an authority figure, even when such actions conflict with their moral beliefs.
   - Milgram's findings have had profound implications for our understanding of authority, conformity, and human behavior, influencing fields such as psychology, ethics, and even legal standards.

5. **Ethical Considerations**:
   - The Milgram experiment is often cited in discussions of research ethics due to the psychological stress experienced by participants. The experiment raised important questions about the ethics of deception in research and the potential harm to participants.

The Milgram experiment remains one of the most famous and controversial studies in the history of psychology, providing deep insights into the darker aspects of human nature and the capacity for obedience to authority.

The lethality of an electric shock depends on several factors, including the voltage, current (measured in amperes or milliamperes), duration of exposure, the path the electricity takes through the body, and individual physiological differences. Voltage alone does not determine lethality; it's the current that passes through the body that is most critical.

### Key Points on Electrical Shock Lethality:

1. **Voltage vs. Current**:
   - Voltage is the potential difference that drives the electric current through the body, but it's the current (measured in amperes or milliamperes) that causes harm or death.
   - A current as low as 100 to 200 milliamperes (mA) passing through the heart can cause ventricular fibrillation, a life-threatening condition that leads to cardiac arrest.

2. **250 Volts**:
   - 250 volts is a high voltage, and contact with it under certain conditions can indeed be lethal, especially if the current is sufficient and the electricity passes through vital organs such as the heart or brain.
   - However, the lethality also depends on the resistance of the body and the conditions of exposure (e.g., dry skin vs. wet skin). Wet skin significantly reduces resistance, allowing more current to flow through the body, which increases the risk of death.

3. **General Guidelines**:
   - **Below 100 Volts**: Generally considered lower risk, but can still be dangerous or lethal under certain conditions, especially in the presence of water or moisture.
   - **100–240 Volts**: Common in household electricity; potentially lethal if the current passes through the heart or brain.
   - **250 Volts and Above**: Typically considered high voltage, with a greater likelihood of lethal outcomes, particularly in cases where the current flow is high enough and the exposure time is prolonged.

4. **Context Matters**:
   - An electric shock from a 250-volt source could be lethal, but the exact outcome depends on various factors such as the condition of the person’s skin, the duration of the shock, and the specific path the current takes through the body.

### Conclusion:
250 volts can be lethal under the right circumstances, particularly if the shock results in a current sufficient to cause ventricular fibrillation or disrupt normal heart function. However, it's not the voltage alone that determines lethality, but the combination of voltage, current, and other factors.
