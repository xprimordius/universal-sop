In contract law, misrepresentation occurs when a false statement induces a party to enter into a contract. Even if a contract is in writing, certain landmark cases have established that oral misrepresentations can override written terms, leading to the contract's rescission or modification. Notable cases include:

1. **Smith v Land and House Property Corporation (1884)**: Here, a seller described a tenant as "most desirable," despite knowing the tenant was in arrears. The court deemed this a misrepresentation, allowing the buyer to rescind the contract. citeturn0search12

2. **Esso Petroleum Co Ltd v Mardon (1976)**: Esso provided a prospective tenant with an overestimated sales forecast for a petrol station. The court held that Esso's statement, given their expertise, constituted a negligent misrepresentation, entitling Mardon to damages. citeturn0search13

3. **Curtis v Chemical Cleaning and Dyeing Co (1951)**: A customer was misled by an assistant about the extent of an exclusion clause in a cleaning contract. The court ruled that the oral misrepresentation nullified the written exclusion, holding the company liable for damages. citeturn0search13

These cases demonstrate that oral misrepresentations can, under certain circumstances, override written contract terms, leading to legal remedies such as rescission or damages.
