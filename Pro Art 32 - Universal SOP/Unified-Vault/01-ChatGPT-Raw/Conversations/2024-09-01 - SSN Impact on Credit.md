# SSN Impact on Credit

---

## 👤 You

if someone gets a new SSN, do they get brand new credit? and no felonies?

---

## 🤖 ChatGPT

If someone receives a new Social Security Number (SSN), whether through a legal process or otherwise, it does not automatically grant them a "clean slate" in terms of credit or criminal history. Here are the key points to consider:

1. **Credit History:**
   - **Linkage to Old SSN:** The new SSN may still be linked to the old one, especially if the change was due to identity theft or other reasons where continuity of credit history is necessary. Credit bureaus might merge the new SSN with the old credit file, meaning the individual's credit history (good or bad) could follow them.
   - **Starting Fresh:** In rare cases, if the new SSN is not linked to the old one, the individual might appear as having no credit history. This can be a double-edged sword: while negative credit history might not follow, the absence of any credit history can make it difficult to obtain loans or credit.

2. **Criminal History:**
   - **Felonies and Criminal Records:** Criminal records are tied to a person’s identity, which includes more than just the SSN. Records are associated with names, birthdates, and other identifying information. Changing an SSN does not erase or affect criminal records. Law enforcement agencies and courts track criminal histories through multiple identifiers, so felonies would still be present in background checks.

**In Summary:** A new SSN does not automatically grant someone brand-new credit or erase felonies. The credit history might carry over, and criminal records will remain accessible through other personal identifiers.

## 🤖 ChatGPT

**Expunging a Felony vs. Removing a Felony:**

**1. Expunging a Felony:**
   - **Definition:** Expungement is a legal process through which a court orders the physical destruction or sealing of records related to a criminal conviction, effectively removing the conviction from public records.
   - **Effect:** Once expunged, the felony conviction is treated as if it never occurred. It will not appear on most background checks, and the individual can legally deny the conviction in most situations (e.g., on job applications).
   - **Limitations:** Expungement does not erase the conviction from all records. Some government agencies, particularly law enforcement, may still have access to the expunged records. Additionally, not all felonies are eligible for expungement; the eligibility depends on the nature of the crime, the jurisdiction, and whether certain conditions (like a waiting period or completion of probation) have been met.

**2. Removing a Felony:**
   - **Definition:** The term "removing a felony" can be misleading, as it generally does not involve the literal removal of a felony from a criminal record. Instead, it might refer to various methods like expungement, sealing, or pardoning.
   - **Effect:** The process and effect of "removing" a felony vary depending on the method used:
     - **Sealing:** Similar to expungement but less thorough. Sealed records are hidden from public view but still exist and can be accessed by certain authorities.
     - **Pardoning:** A pardon is an act of forgiveness by a government official (typically a governor or the president) that can restore certain rights (like voting) but does not erase the conviction from the criminal record. The felony remains on record but may have a notation indicating a pardon was granted.
   - **Limitations:** Unlike expungement, other methods of "removing" a felony typically do not erase the conviction from all records, and the felony may still be visible in certain situations, such as when applying for a government job or professional license.

**Key Differences:**
- **Expungement** effectively erases the conviction for most purposes, while other methods, like sealing or pardoning, may hide or forgive the conviction without erasing it.
- The availability and process for expungement or other forms of record relief vary by jurisdiction, and not all felonies are eligible for these remedies. 

It’s crucial to consult legal advice specific to your jurisdiction to understand the options available for dealing with a felony conviction.
