We revisit an issue first addressed by our court in Nguyen
v. Barnes & Noble, Inc., 763 F.3d 1171 (9th Cir. 2014):
Under what circumstances can the use of a website bind a
consumer to a set of hyperlinked “terms and conditions” that
the consumer never saw or read?
In this case, plaintiffs used defendants’ websites but did
not see a notice in fine print stating, “I understand and agree
to the Terms & Conditions which includes mandatory
arbitration.” When a dispute arose and plaintiffs filed this
lawsuit, defendants moved to compel arbitration, arguing
that plaintiffs’ use of the websites signified their agreement
to the mandatory arbitration provision found in the
hyperlinked terms and conditions. The district court rejected
this argument, and so do we. Plaintiffs did not
unambiguously manifest their assent to the terms and
conditions when navigating through the websites, and as a
result they never entered into a binding agreement to
arbitrate their dispute. We therefore affirm the district

agreement encompasses the dispute at issue.” Lifescan, Inc.
v. Premier Diabetic Services, Inc., 363 F.3d 1010, 1012 (9th

governs “because both California and New York law dictate
the same outcome.” 763 F.3d at 1175.

the parties must manifest their mutual assent to the terms of

2002) (applying California law). Parties traditionally

do so through conduct. Specht, 306 F.3d at 29. However,

continuing to use the website. See Nguyen, 763 F.3d at 1176.

clicking a button or checking a box, that unambiguously

868 F.3d at 75; Nguyen, 763 F.3d at 1173 (refusing to

**Berman v. Freedom Financial Network (9th Cir. 2022) — Publication Summary**

The Ninth Circuit held that the defendants could not force consumers into arbitration based on website terms they never clearly saw or agreed to.

The case involved plaintiffs who used lead-generation websites containing fine-print language stating: “I understand and agree to the Terms & Conditions which includes mandatory arbitration.” That language was placed in tiny gray text, with hyperlinked terms that were not clearly identifiable as links. Users clicked large green “Continue” buttons, but the pages did not clearly say that clicking “Continue” meant they were agreeing to the terms.

The court reaffirmed that online contracts require mutual assent. It explained the difference between:

- **Clickwrap**: usually enforceable, because users must expressly click “I agree”
- **Browsewrap**: often unenforceable, because terms are hidden behind hyperlinks and assent is merely implied by use

The Ninth Circuit held that where a website operator cannot prove the consumer had actual knowledge of the terms, enforceability requires both:

1. **Reasonably conspicuous notice** of the terms  
2. **Unambiguous manifestation of assent** to those terms

The defendants failed both requirements.

On conspicuousness, the court found the notice inadequate because the text was extremely small, visually de-emphasized, and the hyperlinks were not clearly marked. On assent, the court found that clicking “Continue” did not unambiguously show agreement because the site did not explicitly tell users that clicking the button meant they were accepting the terms.

The court therefore held that no binding agreement to arbitrate was formed and affirmed denial of the motion to compel arbitration.

The Ninth Circuit also affirmed denial of reconsideration, holding that defendants waited too long to present deposition testimony they could have submitted earlier.

**Core takeaway for publication:**  
A business cannot bind consumers to arbitration through buried website terms unless the site gives clear, conspicuous notice and explicitly ties the user’s action—such as clicking a button—to agreement with those terms. Fine print and passive design are not enough.
