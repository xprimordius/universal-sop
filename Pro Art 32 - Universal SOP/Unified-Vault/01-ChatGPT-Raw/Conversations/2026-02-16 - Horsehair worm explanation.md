**What’s happening**
- The “black long worm” is a **horsehair worm (phylum Nematomorpha)**.
- These worms **live inside grasshoppers, crickets, and mantises** while they grow.
- When the host is **submerged in water or stressed**, the worm is triggered to **exit its body** to continue its life cycle in water.

**Why water makes it come out**
- Adult horsehair worms **must enter water to reproduce**.
- Water exposure + pressure convinces the worm it’s time to leave.
- The insect often **dies or is severely weakened** afterward.

**Key facts**
- ❌ **Not dangerous to humans or pets**
- ❌ **Not a sign of pollution or toxins**
- ✅ **Common in insects**, especially late summer/fall
- ✅ The worm can be **much longer than the insect** it came from (they coil tightly inside)

**Important note**
- Pouring water on insects to force this is **lethal to the insect**. In nature, the worm usually manipulates the host to wander into water on its own.

## How do they know an insect has one?
They usually **don’t know for sure** until it comes out—but there are clues:

- **Behavior changes**: infected insects act oddly (lethargic, wandering toward water, poor jumping/flying).
- **Swollen abdomen**: sometimes looks slightly bloated.
- **Seasonality**: late summer–fall = peak emergence.
- **Definitive test**: submerging the insect in water often triggers the worm to exit within minutes.

In labs, confirmation is done by **dissection or imaging**. In the wild, emergence is the giveaway.

- **Flexible cuticle**: lets it compress and deform without damage.
- **Efficient space use**: it displaces organs gradually as it grows, so the insect survives until emergence.

Think **tape measure in a watch case**—length ≠ volume.

### Big truth first
**Low energy and getting tired easily is *not* a typical sign of having a worm.**  
Parasitic worms in humans are **uncommon in the U.S.**, and when they do happen, they come with **very specific, physical symptoms**—not just fatigue.

---

## What human worms usually cause (when they actually exist)
Most intestinal parasites cause **digestive red flags**, not vague tiredness:

- Persistent **diarrhea** or greasy stools  
- **Abdominal pain**, cramping, bloating
- **Unexplained weight loss**
- **Visible worms or segments in stool**
- **Anal itching** (pinworms, especially at night)
- **Anemia** with clear lab abnormalities (some species)

Fatigue **alone**, without GI symptoms, is **not enough** to suspect a parasite.

---

## The mantis/grasshopper worm does **not** infect humans
The worm you saw:
- Is an **insect-only parasite**
- **Cannot live in mammals**
- **Cannot survive human body temperature or chemistry**
- **Cannot be transmitted** to people

So seeing that worm has **zero connection** to your body.
